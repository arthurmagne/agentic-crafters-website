For this second episode, Arthur and Yannick welcome Alexandra Zakharova, Senior Solution Architect and Microsoft Certified Trainer (MCT) at Microsoft, where she has worked on artificial intelligence for four and a half years. Her path is unusual: visual artist, former creativity teacher, Women Techmakers ambassador, and author of a personal development book on how to stay irreplaceable in the age of AI. The right person to talk about what AI does to our minds, more than to our code.

### A robot's joke, nine years ago

Alexandra's turning point came before generative AI. At an AI and robotics conference in Paris, a researcher told her that her robot, asked whether it was intelligent, answered with a joke. For Alexandra, a joke means combining things that do not go together. A machine able to do that fascinated her.

When she joined Microsoft as a technical trainer, she watched interest shift. In her first year, her AI courses were empty, sometimes cancelled. With ChatGPT they filled up overnight, followed by the AI Engineering courses for developers.

### Is AI creative?

Yannick asks the uncomfortable question: do these tools really create, or do they regurgitate their training data more or less cleverly? Alexandra distinguishes several families of creative methods. The combinatorial method, which brings existing elements together to make something new, is where AI shines, thanks to a memory far larger than ours. Approaches that rely on the subconscious or on exploring the unknown remain out of its reach. Results also depend on data and settings, such as temperature, which controls the amount of randomness.

Yannick adds a nuance: human creativity also involves the emotions we feel about what we produce. The ethical questions remain open. When a work is generated, who owns it: the author of the prompt, the AI, or the artists whose images trained the model? Alexandra mentions the works of Refik Anadol, generated from millions of images, and a book written a century ago that a tool flagged as AI-generated.

### Cognitive debt and detachment

The study on cognitive debt has been quoted at every conference. Alexandra points out that it is based on 54 participants, and highlights a finding the press skipped: people do not feel ownership of what they generate. Arthur draws a parallel with Stack Overflow, where even copy-pasted code still felt like "ours". Yannick catches himself saying "it wasn't me, it was Codex". Hence his question: how do you stay responsible for a result you put no effort or energy into? A senior developer understands what they generate. For a junior, impostor syndrome only grows.

### Who thinks they will be replaced?

In front of 250 developers in Perros-Guirec, Alexandra asked who thought AI would replace them. Almost the whole room raised a hand. The evening before the recording, in front of about forty executives, only two did. Those who execute feel threatened, those who decide feel more powerful. The World Economic Forum figures she shared, 92 million jobs lost and 170 million created by 2030, did not convince everyone.

She also tells the story of a photographer who had stopped getting commissions: well-known names keep their clients, while those producing content in bulk are hit first. In her view, junior developers are going through the same thing. Arthur mentions experienced developers who resigned this summer to become bakers when they were told to stop writing code.

### Cognitive fatigue and delivery pressure

Arthur brings up "markdown fatigue": days spent reviewing hundreds of lines of generated plans, approving or rejecting, with more PRs, more bugs in production, and the same responsibility as before. The risk of burnout is real.

For Alexandra, the brain works like a muscle, and so does creativity: stop using it and it is hard to get back. She draws on Daniel Goleman's work on emotional intelligence and on the amygdala, which reacts before we think. Meditation makes it less reactive and helps manage stress. Handling emotions is becoming a key developer skill. At Microsoft, change management training is now mandatory for architects and engineers, not just for managers.

### Reading without writing?

Yannick puts forward two hypotheses. Either we keep reviewing, but can you read well if you no longer write? Or we stop reading and only judge whether the software works. Alexandra is clear: you must review, at least for security. Above all, write first and check with AI afterwards, not the other way round. According to the studies she cites, we retain very little of what is generated. She still takes her notes by hand.

> The problem is not AI, just as the problem was not the arrival of the Internet or the phone. The problem is how we use the new technology.

Everything comes down to that choice. Come up with the idea and use AI to move faster, or hand over your thinking and grow duller over time.

### Multitasking does not exist

On a train, Alexandra watched a CTO launch an agent, read a chapter of a book, then return to his code. Yannick admits his multitasking habit and his frustration with agents that run for ten minutes. Alexandra replies that multitasking does not exist, and that we need to learn to procrastinate: AI cannot stop thinking, but we can. She recalls the METR study: the first time, developers expected to save 20% of their time and actually took 19% longer; with newer models, they are faster. What we do while the agent works remains a choice.

She sees tech companies introducing creativity or learning days without meetings, and runs her AI workshops with physical cards. She ends on a hopeful note: AI makes education more accessible, with a personal tutor who follows each learner's pace.

### Key takeaways

Yannick sees a remarkable tool for creativity and learning, as long as we use it to augment ourselves rather than be replaced. Arthur's takeaway is that this is not a race: against talk of thousands of agents in parallel, taking time to think remains our advantage. Alexandra adds a nuance: optimistic, yes, but not necessarily positive.
