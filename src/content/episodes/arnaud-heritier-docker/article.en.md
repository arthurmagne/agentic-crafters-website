For this first episode, Yannick and Arthur welcome Arnaud Héritier, Engineering Manager at Docker, formerly at Doctolib, and a familiar voice on the French Java podcast Les Cast Codeurs. Twenty-five years in IT, one foot in management and the other still in code: the right person to take a snapshot of what AI is really changing in engineering teams, in the autumn of 2026.

### A manager who codes again

The first change is very concrete: reporting. Getting a piece of information used to take days of scripts, SQL and API calls. Today a small agent produces a report in minutes. Many of these reports are disposable, built to settle a one-off question, and that is exactly what makes them valuable: decisions come faster.

The second change is more personal. Like many engineering managers, Arnaud was buried in meetings and had stopped coding, because he could no longer find long stretches of focus. AI now holds the focus for him. He delegates small tools and prototypes, and has found again an ability to build that he thought he had lost. Teams feel the same effect: they can test a working mini-application in a few hours instead of getting a mock-up approved by thirty-six people. The feedback loop is now measured in days.

### Producing more, absorbing less

Yannick points out the flip side: when producing costs nothing, we produce too much. Reports generated by some, summarised by someone else's AI, and texts that are flawless yet tiring to read. Arnaud agrees and goes further: the problem affects everything AI generates, documentation, code and tests alike. Every test carries a maintenance and CI cost, even when AI writes it.

> If you ask the AI, it will do everything for you. It will even do more.

The answer is still human: knowing which indicators really matter, and framing the AI so it delivers three useful points instead of an endless report. As Arthur notes, this is where the pragmatism of software craftsmanship still makes all the difference.

### Adopting AI takes quarters

At Doctolib, around 500 developers, a three-person team cleared the ground: choosing the first tools (Claude Code at the time), then building the whole ecosystem around them, skills, plugins and marketplaces. Adopting a tool means adopting its ecosystem.

Change management, though, takes time. People need training and explanations, reassurance when they fear for their jobs, and a real hearing for their environmental concerns. Early tools produced mediocre code, which did not help. But the conclusion at Doctolib and at Docker is the same: after a few quarters, nearly everyone gets on board, at their own pace. At Docker today, everyone codes with AI, and also uses it for PR reviews, reporting and the rest of engineering work.

To keep up with a market where a new model ships almost every week, Docker shares findings in internal channels and invests heavily in evaluation: of models, of skills, and of Gordon, the AI agent built into Docker's products that Arnaud works on.

### Context, what AI lacks

The word keeps coming back in Arnaud's answers. An LLM knows neither your company's constraints nor what your team can actually do. It still estimates a feature in person-days, just like the data it was trained on. For Yannick, the real difference is also emotional: our architecture choices come from experience, from a talk that stuck with us, from an intuition that "simple" cannot be reduced to a rule.

Arnaud adds a key point: even with a million tokens of context, someone has to choose what goes in. Our brain naturally reaches for infrastructure cost, schedule and human constraints. Skills and MCP only partly solve this. Providing the right information at the right time is now a large share of the value of whoever drives the AI.

### The bottleneck has moved

Writing code has become fast. The rest of the chain has not kept up. CI is a bottleneck in every company Arnaud talks to, Docker included. Hour-long test suites, slow environments, end-to-end tests that are still slow and expensive: all the tooling designed for humans who produced code slowly needs rethinking.

### Code review is about trust

Faced with huge pull requests, line-by-line review no longer holds. Arnaud admits this saddens him, as someone who loved understanding every detail. But the goal of a review, he reminds us, is confidence in what ships. That confidence can come from elsewhere: smaller stacked PRs, an in-house review bot fed with project context, and classic checks that keep their full value, tests, linters, security scanners, SonarQube.

What gets lost is the knowledge sharing that reviews used to provide. Yannick pushes his concern further: if one day nobody understands a million generated lines, who will open the hood? Arnaud has no final answer. He argues for staying on technologies you know well enough to troubleshoot, and mentions teams that set aside a few days without AI to keep learning. Arthur doubts most people will keep that discipline while competitors speed up.

### The job in 2030

For Arnaud, what stays critical is architecture and broad computing knowledge. Understanding how systems fit together, where the security and operational risks are, and making the calls: multi-zone or not, cloud or not, which language gives a fast feedback loop. He writes a lot of Go at Docker these days, and enjoys it.

> Engineers have always been there to connect the end user, the human, with technology.

Languages may matter less, specifications and tests more. But turning a need into a solution remains real engineering work.

### Key takeaways

A lot of pragmatism, a human role that stays central, and a delivery chain that still needs reinventing beyond code. And one sentence that struck Yannick: we still work for humans. In a world where AIs summarise reports written by other AIs, it may be the question to keep in mind.
